# Verdict on the DX axis — best developer experience, and hygiene-dressed-as-experience

The tell for this axis: sort every proposed change into **what the developer USING fluidbox feels** (a clipped nav, a black 404, a "No runs yet." that's actually an outage, light browser chrome in dark mode, a run silently blocking on a human) versus **what only the codebase author feels** (three token layers → one, a 7,950-line file → fourteen, a stylelint gate, dead-class purge, byte-identical snapshot proofs). All three docs are ~70% the second kind. They differ in how much of the *right* first kind they carry, how early they ship it, and how honestly they label the rest.

## Proposal 1 (strict) — 6/10

Its thesis is "make a wrong value impossible to write down." That is an answer to a *different* question than mine. It's the most honest of the three about being hygiene-first (it never once calls its token-closure work "experience"), which is exactly why it doesn't sink lower — it isn't dressing anything up. But on *felt* DX it makes **zero** of the polish catches: it misses the dark-mode `themeColor`, the dead CJK glyphs, the missing dashboard skip-link, the nav-model incoherence, and live-region announcement entirely. Its state system and masthead are excellent and specific — but those are table stakes all three share.

- **Strongest idea (by this axis):** collapsing the nav to the disclosure menu at **900px, not 1280** — with an actual fit arithmetic (852px usable vs 828px content at 900). It is the only one of the three that keeps the *full inline nav across the entire 900–1280 laptop band*, which is the widest inline-nav range on real screens. A developer in a 1280px window keeps every destination one click away. That is a concrete felt win the other two throw away.
- **Worst idea:** the `Button`/`Card` primitives with **no `className` and no `style` escape hatch** — closed unions only, "an off-scale value is a compile error." This is sold as a DX guarantee but it's ergonomics for the person *writing* fluidbox's UI, not the person *using* it, and it will fight the first one-off (icon-only, full-width, inline-with-text button) into either a variant explosion or a fork. Strictness that removes the author's escape hatch is the opposite of good DX for the only developer it touches.

## Proposal 2 (safe) — 5/10

Genuinely strong *migration* engineering, and structurally the **furthest** from my axis. Its organizing principle is "zero visible regression," which means a large share of its proof-points (`snapshots byte-identical`, `getComputedStyle unchanged`, "proves consolidation is a render no-op") literally certify that **the user feels nothing**. That is the correct goal for a refactor and the wrong goal for a DX brief. It also catches the fewest felt bugs (misses `themeColor`, CJK, nav-model, the login-title override) and its one differentiator — the Phase 0 Playwright golden net — is invisible engineering process.

- **Strongest idea:** "build the net before the trapeze" — capturing visual goldens + violation counters in Phase 0 *before* the three dangerous mechanical edits (delete 8 breakpoints, collapse 15 radii, split a 7,950-line sheet), so a mistake trips a guard *in its own commit*. It's hygiene, but it's the right hygiene, and it's the single best-argued idea in any of the three docs for protecting the locked identity through the churn. I'd steal it wholesale.
- **Worst idea:** sequencing **reduced-motion and the dashboard skip-link into Phase 8 "hardening"** — dead last, behind the entire invisible token/split machinery. Those are the two changes a keyboard user or a motion-sensitive user *feels immediately* (confirmed finding #3: kernel.css has zero `prefers-reduced-motion` blocks). Putting felt accessibility at the back of the queue behind byte-identical token consolidation is the DX-priority inversion of the whole proposal captured in one scheduling decision.

## Proposal 3 (dx) — 8/10

The only proposal *organized* around felt moments ("a developer reads dense machine data, hits failure states constantly, lives on the keyboard"), and the only one that earns it: every polish catch it makes that the others miss, I verified as real — the dark-mode `themeColor` painting light browser chrome (layout.tsx:60), the Noto JP/SC fonts loaded `subsets:["latin"]` so the CJK title echoes have no glyphs (layout.tsx:21-33), the dashboard missing the skip-link the marketing shell already has, the Overview success-rate `—` overloaded across loading/offline/zero, and reduced-motion foregrounded as a contract rather than deferred. Crucially, it **sequences the visible wins first** ("Ship WP4/WP5 as soon as WP1–3 land — they are the visible DX wins and shouldn't wait on the module split") — the exact inverse of P2. And it's the most honest about the hygiene/experience split: it labels its own module-split and lint work as "makes the code navigable / makes it permanent," not as experience, and explicitly flags its spacing/type/motion scales as scope additions "so the owner can veto."

- **Strongest idea (and the most DX-attuned sentence in all three docs):** treating **failure and liveness as first-class experience** — `ApiError.kind` feeding `StateError` so a deleted run reads "this resource no longer exists" with *no* Retry while an outage reads "unreachable" *with* Retry (killing the confirmed `sessions/[id]` bug where a 404 shows a permanent outage), plus `role="log"/aria-live="polite"` on the run timeline and `role="alert"` on the pending-approval banner because "a run blocked on a human is the most announce-worthy event in the product." For a *governance* product whose entire reason to exist is the human-approval pause, making that pause the thing that gets announced is the single best product-DX insight on the table.
- **Worst idea:** collapsing the whole nav to a hamburger **below 1280px**. P3 adopts the strongest anti-clip invariant of the three — `minmax(max-content, 1fr)` genuinely makes an internally-clipped nav item structurally impossible, better than P1/P2's arithmetic — and then throws away the density payoff by hamburgering ~400px too early. A developer in any non-maximized 1200–1279 window loses one-click Governance/Recipes/Settings and must open a menu, a felt regression that P1 *avoids on the same axis P3 champions*. Its `max-content` floor didn't need a 1280 collapse; it could have held the inline nav far lower. (Its chrome IA spends — avatar menu, de-duped New Run, nav demotion — brush "no new layout language," but those are defensible; the premature hamburger is the real self-inflicted felt loss. And its spacing scale justified as "dense-data legibility" is the one place P3 itself dresses hygiene as experience — a 22px→24px snap changes no legibility — though it flags it for veto.)

## Build on Proposal 3.

It is the only one that starts from the developer's felt moments instead of the codebase's tidiness, its unique catches are all verified real, and it ships the visible wins before the invisible ones. But graft three things on:

1. **Take P1's masthead density decision.** Keep the inline nav down to **900**, not 1280 — fold P3's `minmax(max-content,1fr)` non-clip *invariant* into P1's *keep-inline-to-900* threshold, so you get both the structural no-clip guarantee and the widest inline-nav range. This is strictly better than either proposal's masthead alone.
2. **Take P2's Phase 0 golden net** for the risky token/split mechanical edits — P3's per-WP measurements are good but P2's before-the-churn baseline is the stronger guard for identity preservation.
3. **Make the owner explicitly veto or approve P3's chrome IA** (avatar/initials menu, nav-model demotion) rather than shipping them by default — they're the one place P3 quietly spends identity-scope budget the brief withheld.

**Blunt bottom line:** P1 is the best *engineering* doc and answers the wrong question; P2 is the safest doc and its safety is antithetical to DX; P3 is 70% the same hygiene as the others with the *right* 30% of felt-DX bolted on top and sequenced first — which, on this axis, is the whole ballgame.
